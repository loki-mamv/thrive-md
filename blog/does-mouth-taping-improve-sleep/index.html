Does Mouth Taping Improve Sleep?

October 20, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Probably not for most people. A 2025 systematic review in PLOS ONE found 10 small studies, all rated poor quality, and only two showed a real drop in sleep apnea scores. Those two involved people with mild sleep apnea who breathe through their mouths. The authors concluded the data do not support mouth taping as a treatment and warned of possible harm.

What is mouth taping, and why do people try it?

Mouth taping means placing a strip of tape, or a purpose-made patch, over the lips before sleep. The goal is to keep the mouth closed so you breathe through your nose all night.

The idea spread on social media. A 2025 scoping review in the American Journal of Otolaryngology looked at 50 TikTok videos on the topic. The most common claimed benefits were better sleep and better oral health. Other claims included more energy and stronger immunity. The authors noted that few of these claims had ever been tested.

There is a sensible idea underneath the trend. Nasal breathing is the normal route during sleep. A 2022 study from Chang Gung Memorial Hospital notes that mouth breathing narrows the upper airway and can make obstructive sleep apnea worse. The open question is whether tape is a safe or useful way to change it.

What does the research actually show?

The best summary so far is the PLOS ONE review by Jess Rhee, Brian Rotenberg, and colleagues at London Health Sciences Centre in Ontario. Here is what they found:

  • They screened 86 records and included 10 studies, with a little over 200 participants in total.
  • Individual studies enrolled between 9 and 71 people. Only one was a randomized controlled trial.
  • All 10 studies were rated poor quality on a standard bias scale, so the team did not pool the results.
  • Six studies measured the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), the count of breathing pauses per hour. Only two found a significant drop.

Their conclusion was blunt: "The existing data does not support mouth taping or oral occlusion as a sound clinical intervention."

The earlier scoping review reached a softer view. It found nine relevant studies and called the evidence "markedly heterogeneous," with little agreement on benefits. One study of mouth taping in asthma found no benefit. Both reviews agree that better trials are needed.

Does it help snoring or mild sleep apnea?

There is a small signal in one narrow group: people with mild sleep apnea who breathe through their mouths and have clear noses.

The key study is a 2022 preliminary study of 20 mouth breathers with mild obstructive sleep apnea. Each person had a home sleep test, then a second test a week later with their mouth taped. Results:

  • Median AHI fell from 8.3 to 4.7 events per hour.
  • The median snoring index fell by 47%.
  • 13 of the 20 people (65%) cut their snoring index by at least half.
  • The lowest oxygen level during sleep rose from 82.5% to 87%.

That sounds promising, but the study had no control group and only 20 people. Participants were also chosen because they could tolerate the tape. A 2026 meta-analysis in OTO Open found only two mouth taping studies usable, too few to pool. It described them in words only.

Results are less clear in more severe disease. A small Taiwanese study filmed people with sleep apnea while their mouths were taped. Many "mouth puffed," forcing air out past the tape. People with severe apnea showed different puffing patterns than people with milder disease. Tape does not simply switch the airway to the nose.

Is mouth taping safe?

For some people, it may not be. The PLOS ONE authors wrote that there is "a potentially serious risk of harm for individuals indiscriminately practicing this trend." Four of the 10 studies they reviewed discussed risks, including asphyxiation in people with a blocked nose. The authors of the mild sleep apnea study advised against taping in moderate to severe disease.

The two main concerns are:

  • A blocked nose. A cold, allergies, a deviated septum, or nasal polyps can close the nasal airway. Tape removes the backup route through the mouth.
  • Hidden sleep apnea. Tape can quiet snoring without fixing the breathing pauses behind it. That can delay a diagnosis.

Nasal blockage matters a lot. The OTO Open meta-analysis found that a blocked nose raised AHI by about 14 events per hour. In some healthy people, it produced patterns that looked like sleep apnea.

Why can snoring be a sign of something more serious?

Snoring is the most common warning sign of obstructive sleep apnea. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) compares it to a fire alarm that demands attention. The AASM says untreated sleep apnea raises the risk of heart disease, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, depression, and death.

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute lists these signs of sleep apnea:

  • Frequent loud snoring
  • Breathing that stops and starts, or gasping for air
  • Daytime sleepiness and fatigue
  • Dry mouth and headaches
  • Waking often at night to urinate

Waking with a dry mouth is often the reason people reach for tape. But dry mouth is itself a possible sign of sleep apnea. Ongoing daytime sleepiness also deserves attention (see our article on whether long daytime naps are a warning sign).

The AASM advises anyone who snores, or whose partner snores, to talk to a doctor. If sleep apnea is suspected, the next step is an in-lab sleep study or a home sleep apnea test.

What can you try instead of tape?

If your goal is better sleep, start with the basics that have stronger evidence:

  1. Get snoring checked. A sleep test can rule sleep apnea in or out. Proven treatments include CPAP, oral appliances, and positional therapy.
  2. Treat a blocked nose. An ear, nose, and throat doctor can look for allergies, a deviated septum, or polyps that force mouth breathing.
  3. Fix your sleep habits. A regular schedule and a cool, dark room help more than most gadgets. Our guide to common sleep hygiene mistakes covers the details.

If your main problem is trouble falling or staying asleep, tape is unlikely to help. insomnia.md covers evidence-based treatment for insomnia, including cognitive behavioral therapy.

If you still want to try taping, talk to a doctor first. Ask whether you need a sleep test, and make sure you can breathe freely through your nose.

The bottom line

Mouth taping has weak evidence behind it. A few small studies suggest it may cut snoring and breathing pauses in people with mild sleep apnea who breathe through their mouths. For everyone else, there is no good proof it improves sleep. It can also hide sleep apnea or be risky with a blocked nose. If you snore, get evaluated before you tape.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is mouth tape the same as a chin strap?

No. Tape seals the lips, while a chin strap holds the jaw closed. The 2025 PLOS ONE review included both types of device and found weak evidence for each. Neither is a proven treatment for sleep apnea.

Can mouth taping cure sleep apnea?

No. The best study found that median breathing pauses fell by about half in 20 people with mild sleep apnea, but the study had no control group. The study authors advised against taping in moderate to severe sleep apnea. Proven options include CPAP, oral appliances, and positional therapy.

Should children use mouth tape?

The studies in the 2025 review enrolled adults, with average ages from about 38 to 64. There is no good evidence on safety or benefit in children. A child who snores or breathes through the mouth at night should see a pediatrician or ear, nose, and throat doctor.

How do I know if I breathe through my mouth at night?

Common clues are waking with a dry mouth, a sore throat, or drool on the pillow, and a bed partner who notices your mouth open. Dry mouth is also a listed sign of sleep apnea, so it is worth mentioning to a doctor.

Is mouth taping safe with a cold or allergies?

It is riskier. A blocked nose removes your main airway, and tape removes the backup route through the mouth. A 2026 meta-analysis found that nasal blockage alone raised breathing pauses by about 14 events per hour.
