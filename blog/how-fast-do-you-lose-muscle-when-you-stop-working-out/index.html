How Fast Do You Lose Muscle When You Stop Working Out?

October 10, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Slower than most people fear, unless you are in bed or in a cast. In a study of 20 trained young men, two weeks without lifting cost no leg press strength and no significant lean mass. Ten weeks off does shrink muscle and strength, but a 2024 Finnish trial found both came back within about five weeks of retraining. Full disuse is a different case: a leg cast cut thigh muscle size by 3.5% in five days.

What happens in the first two to three weeks off?

Very little, if you stay on your feet. The best direct test is a Baylor University study of 20 resistance-trained men, average age about 21. They lifted four days a week for four weeks, stopped for two weeks, then trained four more weeks. Leg press strength did not drop after the two-week break. Lean mass measured by DXA scan did not fall significantly either.

A three-week break also looks safe for long-term progress. In a six-month Japanese trial, 14 young men did bench press training three days a week. Half trained without a break for 24 weeks. The other half did six-week blocks with three-week breaks between them. Gains in chest and triceps muscle size and in bench press strength were similar in the two groups at the end. The break group trained for fewer weeks and reached the same place.

Two limits apply. Both studies were small, and both enrolled young men. They do not tell us how a 70-year-old responds to the same break.

What happens after two to five months off?

Now the losses show up on a scan. The Finnish trial randomized 55 untrained adults, average age 32, and 42 finished. One group trained twice a week for 10 weeks, stopped for 10 weeks, then trained 10 more weeks. During the 10 weeks off, leg press and biceps curl strength fell. Thigh and biceps muscle size fell too. The abstract does not give those losses as percentages.

Longer breaks can erase size gains completely in new lifters. In a Scandinavian study, 19 men and women trained one leg for 10 weeks and then stopped for 20 weeks. Muscle thickness had risen about 10% and fiber area about 17% with training. Both returned to baseline after the 20 weeks off. Strength did not: it stayed above the starting level.

They were beginners who lost recent gains. The studies report a return toward the starting level, not a drop below it.

Do you lose strength or muscle size first?

It depends on why you stopped.

  • You stopped lifting but stayed active. Size goes first and strength lasts longer. The 20-week study above shows the pattern. A University of Alabama at Birmingham trial of 70 adults found the same. After 16 weeks of training, strength was largely retained through 32 weeks of detraining, with only a slight drop at the last test. The authors of the 20-week study credit a long-lasting motor learning effect: the nervous system keeps the skill of the lift.
  • You are in bed or in a cast. The order flips. A systematic review of bed rest studies pooled 318 healthy adults across 5 to 120 days. Strength fell faster than muscle size during the first two weeks. Both dropped fastest in the first days and then slowed. Muscle shrinkage explained about 79% of the strength loss.

Across all kinds of breaks, the longer the break, the larger the strength loss. A meta-analysis of 103 studies found that dose-response link. It also found a larger effect in people over 65 and in people who were otherwise inactive.

How fast does muscle shrink with bed rest or a cast?

Fast enough to measure in days. In a Maastricht University study, 24 healthy men aged about 23 wore a full leg cast on one leg. The results:

  • After 5 days: quadriceps cross-sectional area fell 3.5% and strength fell 9.0%.
  • After 14 days: quadriceps area fell 8.4%, leg lean mass fell 3.1%, and strength fell 22.9%.

Compare that with the two-week gym break, where trained men lost no leg press strength. The difference is load. A person on a gym break still walks, climbs stairs and carries things. A leg in a cast does none of that.

This is why an injury or a hospital stay calls for a plan. Ask your care team which movements are safe during recovery.

Do older adults lose muscle faster?

With bed rest, yes. In a University of Utah study, nine older and 14 young adults spent five days in bed. Leg lean mass and strength fell in the older adults but not in the young ones. Eight weeks of high-intensity resistance exercise then restored both in the older group.

A second study shows the size of the loss. Eleven healthy adults aged about 67 stayed in bed for 10 days. Knee extensor strength fell 13.2%, stair-climbing power fell 14%, and aerobic capacity fell 12%.

With a simple training break, older adults hold on longer than you might expect. A 2022 meta-analysis pooled six studies of older adults who stopped resistance training. The drop in muscle size was not statistically significant after 12 to 24 weeks off. It was significant after 31 to 52 weeks off. The number of studies is small, so treat the exact cutoffs with care.

Muscle strength in later life tracks with broader health. See our article on what grip strength predicts about health. We also cover creatine for older adults.

How quickly does muscle come back?

Faster than it was built the first time, in the best trial so far. In the Finnish study, the group that took 10 weeks off regained size and strength quickly. In the first five weeks of retraining, their gains in leg press strength and in thigh and biceps size were greater than gains in the continuous group over matching weeks. Both groups ended with similar results. The authors wrote that trainees "should not be too concerned about occasional short-term training breaks."

The cause of this "muscle memory" is not settled. One idea is that muscle fibers keep the extra nuclei they gained from training. A 2024 study of 12 adults found those nuclei did persist through 16 weeks of detraining. It did not find clearly better regrowth in the previously trained arm. The 20-week detraining study also found no retraining advantage in the previously trained leg. Fast regain is observed. The mechanism is still under study.

How little training keeps your muscle?

Much less than it took to build it. In the Alabama trial, adults trained three days a week for 16 weeks. Then some cut their weekly training to one-third or one-ninth of that amount for 32 weeks. Both reduced amounts preserved muscle size in adults aged 20 to 35. Neither preserved it in adults aged 60 to 75.

A 2021 narrative review summed up the research this way:

  • Younger adults: strength and size can hold for up to 32 weeks with one session a week and one set per exercise.
  • Older adults: holding muscle size may take up to two sessions a week and two to three sets per exercise.
  • Both groups: the weight on the bar must stay heavy. Intensity matters more than frequency or volume.

The older-adult figure matches the federal baseline. The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans say adults need muscle-strengthening activity at least two days each week.

The bottom line

A two- to three-week break while you stay active costs little or nothing in the studies available. Ten to 20 weeks off shrinks muscle, and strength holds longer than size. Regain is quick once you start again. Bed rest and casts are the real threat: one study measured a 3.5% loss of thigh muscle size in five days. Older adults lose more with bed rest and need more training to maintain size. If you cannot train fully, one or two short, heavy sessions a week can hold most of what you built.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will I lose muscle if I take one week off from the gym?

The studies here did not test a one-week break. Trained young men who stopped lifting for two weeks lost no leg press strength and no significant lean mass in a 2017 study. A shorter break while you stay active is unlikely to do more.

Why do my muscles look smaller after a few days off?

The studies in this article measured muscle with ultrasound, DXA scans and biopsies, not with a mirror. Those tools found no significant loss in trained men after two weeks off. How a muscle looks from day to day was not tested.

Does walking prevent muscle loss during a break from lifting?

No trial here tested walking alone as a way to hold muscle. The contrast between studies does point to daily load as the key. Men on a two-week gym break kept their strength, and men with a leg in a cast for two weeks lost 22.9% of it.

How long does it take to regain lost muscle?

In a 2024 Finnish trial, adults who stopped for 10 weeks regained their lost size and strength within about five weeks of retraining. They finished level with a group that never took a break. These were new lifters aged about 32, so results in other groups may differ.

Is cardio fitness lost faster than strength?

The research here covers muscle size and strength, not a direct comparison with cardio. One bed rest study of adults aged about 67 found aerobic capacity fell 12% and knee strength fell 13.2% in 10 days. That is bed rest, not a normal training break.
